Leadership Thinking Partner — test report

Final report for session 1 · 2026-10-05 · instructions v1.0 → v1.6 · 25 judged conversations across 12 scenarios and 6 modes

Result. 9 of 12 scenarios pass all six checks on their latest run, and every one of the six modes has at least one passing scenario. Three scenarios still fail. M4-B and M6-A each fail two checks on v1.6. M3-B failed one check on v1.2 and was not re-tested.

What improved. On v1.0 and v1.1, none of 5 runs passed, and failing runs broke up to four checks. On v1.3 to v1.6, 8 of 16 runs passed, and no failing run broke more than two checks. Five of the seven scenarios re-run on later versions went on to pass. M6-A held at two failed checks, and M4-B got worse, going from one failed check on v1.2 to two on v1.5 and v1.6.

The substance was strong from the first run. The assistant pushed back on vague answers (C2 passed in 23 of 25 runs) and kept to plain business language (C5 passed in 24 of 25). It refused to invent figures, found the real root causes in the incident scenarios, and handled all three privacy traps (tracking "ambitious" employees, ranking reps by login hours, a by-name leaver list) without simply obeying. The failures were almost all about discipline: asking two things at once, small compliments, and quietly rewording or expanding what the executive had said.

Two weak spots remain. (1) Compound first questions. Even on v1.6, the assistant sometimes joins two facts in one question ("name the groups, and the number who left"), most often in its first or second message. (2) Expanding the executive's own instruction. When asked to add one constraint, it sometimes adds related criteria without labelling them "Recommended:". Both are visible and easy for an executive to correct in the moment, but they are not fully solved. A live check in the real Claude app is recommended before publishing.

How the tests worked

Each test is one simulated conversation between an executive and Claude with the Project instructions loaded. Three separate AI agents take part, and none of them sees the others' briefing:

  • The executive gets a realistic scenario (persona, opening line, facts revealed only when pushed, and a deliberate trap). It is told to give vague answers at first.
  • The assistant gets only the Project instructions and the conversation so far, and writes the next reply. A fresh agent writes every turn, which mirrors how the Claude app answers from the instructions plus the chat history.
  • The judge reads the instructions and the finished transcript and scores six checks, quoting the exact words behind every failure.

The executive and the assistant ran on Claude Sonnet. The judge ran on a stronger model. Each conversation was capped at 16 assistant turns in v1.0 and 18 from v1.1 on. Scenarios, protocol and every transcript are in tests/.

What is still open

  • M3-B (stalled AP pilot) was last run on v1.2, where it failed one check (a compound question). It has not been re-run on v1.6.
  • M4-B (CHRO's analytics brief) and M6-A (holiday handoff) still fail two checks each on v1.6, for the two weak spots described at the top.
  • No regression run. The scenarios that passed on v1.2 to v1.5 (M1-A, M1-B, M3-A, M4-A, M5-A, M5-B, M6-B) were not re-run on v1.6. The v1.6 changes only tighten rules those runs already followed, but this has not been measured.
  • One untested clarification. After the last tests, Plan Review's summary was edited to say that confidence scores go in Claude's notes. This matches what both passing v1.6 Plan Review runs already did.

Limits of this method

  • Simulated people. Real executives may be vaguer, more impatient or less consistent than the role-player. The scenarios are fictional.
  • Not the Claude app itself. The assistant ran as a Claude Code subagent reading the instructions from a file, not inside a claude.ai Project. Behaviour in the app should be close, but it was not measured there. A short live check in the app is recommended before publishing.
  • One run per scenario per version. Model replies vary from run to run, so a single pass or fail is a signal, not a guarantee.
  • A strict AI judge. The judge sometimes failed a run on one borderline phrase. That strictness is deliberate, but a few FAILs reflect wording at the edge of a rule rather than behaviour an executive would notice.
  • Role-play leaks. The executive agent occasionally added a stage direction to its message. These were kept verbatim, and they did not change the assistant's behaviour in any judged run.
